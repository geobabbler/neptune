## Three Topics That Stood Out

**1. Washington Shifts Its Gaze From Pixels to Models**

GeoAI and the Law's deep dive covers the GITS Act of 2026, which Representative John Moolenaar introduced on November 17. The bill would have NGA assess commercially available AI tools for automated imagery analysis within 90 days, with specific attention to tools lacking meaningful access restrictions, and pass its unclassified findings to Commerce for export control review. Kevin Pomfret notes that a narrow 2020 control on neural network imagery software lapsed in January 2023, and that self-service APIs, free tiers and open-weight models are exactly what NGA would be asked to examine. The same issue reports that the House passed the Earth MRI Reauthorization 396 to 14, requiring mapping contractors to disclose ownership and favoring U.S.-owned firms. Spatial Source covered an Australia and UK communique calling for closer cooperation on EO and PNT. Geospatial1 relayed Iran's space agency chief saying more than 35 satellites are under construction there.

*Why this matters:* The sovereignty thread's procurement phase has reached software. If openness becomes a risk factor, open geospatial foundation models are the obvious target. GeoAI built for agriculture or insurance could get swept up by definitions written with order-of-battle analysis in mind.

**2. Public Data Isn't the Same as Open Data**

geoObserver vented about German postcodes. A LinkedIn post announced that PLZ data had become public data, but what's on offer turned out to be anything but open, the same story the blog keeps seeing with ALKIS parcel numbers, which are still restricted in Bavaria. The author asks who benefits, besides those still profiting from quasi-public data. Pflugerville, Texas pushed the other way on control. Its council unanimously passed the SAFE Act, which bars vendors from selling city data or using it for unrelated purposes, including training general-purpose AI models, without council approval. It followed concern over the city's Flock camera program and restricts facial recognition. EarthStuff pointed to a quieter kind of public data, the USGS data preservation program. The agency holds over 9,900 rock cores and cuttings from 53,000 wells across 36 states, more than 252 million linear feet of drilling, which could help find overlooked critical minerals.

*Why this matters:* The open data orthodoxy is fraying from both ends. Governments call restricted data public, while cities claw back reuse rights from vendors. Pflugerville's AI training clause is a licensing problem for anyone building models on municipal imagery and LiDAR, and it won't be the last.

**3. Mapping Hazards Before and After They Land**

Maps Mania featured two interactive maps tracking possible lead dust after the US Army detonated 68 drums of degraded lead azide, roughly 9,500 pounds, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown. One of them, Where the Lead Will Go, simulates millions of airborne particles across five days of weather forecasts. Geography Realm covered a new dataset of 170 Landsat-based maps tracking 25 years of fractures on nine Antarctic ice shelves, including Pine Island, Thwaites and Brunt. EarthStuff shared a paper arguing that hazardous glacial lakes, thousands of them in the Himalayas alone, deserve the kind of safety oversight engineered dams have had for over a century. It also resurfaced a 2016 USGS review of karst mapping, which puts average US sinkhole collapse costs above $300 million a year, and a machine learning landslide susceptibility study along Nagaland's NH-2 and NH-29 road corridors.

*Why this matters:* This is applied work the feeds usually skip. Sinkhole losses of $300 million a year are an insurance problem as much as a geology one, and regulating glacial lakes would create a monitoring market. That's the commercial demand side this ecosystem rarely shows.
